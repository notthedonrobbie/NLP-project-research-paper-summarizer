# PaperTrail: An Intelligent Research Assistant for Scientific Document Summarization and Question Answering

## Abstract

Scientific papers contain densely structured knowledge, but reviewing their objectives, methods, datasets, results, and limitations is time-consuming, while ungrounded language-model answers can obscure the origin of claims. This project presents **PaperTrail**, a Python and Streamlit research assistant that extracts text from scientific PDFs, produces structured research briefs, retrieves relevant passages, and answers questions with page-level evidence. The implementation combines page-aware preprocessing, overlapping chunks, LEAD and TextRank extractive summarization, BM25 and TF-IDF retrieval, optional sentence embeddings and BART, and an offline evidence-quoting answer generator. A reproducible pilot used three publicly accessible NLP papers, comprising 42 pages and 205 chunks, with independently written summaries, 15 answerable questions, and three deliberately unanswerable questions. TF-IDF achieved mean Precision@5 of 0.333 and Recall@5 of 0.678, compared with 0.267 and 0.583 for BM25. TextRank obtained slightly lower ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-2 than LEAD but higher ROUGE-L. The final system correctly rejected all three unsupported questions, although it also over-refused one answerable question and often produced incomplete answers. These results demonstrate a working, inspectable prototype rather than a generally superior research assistant. The main value is transparent navigation of source documents, supported by explicit evaluation of retrieval quality, answer reliability, ethical risks, and remaining limitations.

## 1. Problem Definition, NLP Justification, and Value

The target problem is the difficulty of extracting reliable, task-specific information from full-length scientific documents. A literature reviewer rarely needs only a generic abstract: one question concerns the dataset, another concerns the experimental setup, and another asks whether the authors acknowledged a limitation. Ordinary keyword search requires the user to guess the document’s terminology and returns locations without synthesizing an answer. General-purpose generative systems can produce fluent responses from parametric knowledge, but fluency does not establish that a claim occurs in a selected paper. PaperTrail therefore frames the task as **document-conditioned information access**: every summary or answer should remain connected to extracted source text. This scope deliberately excludes broad web question answering and concentrates on English, digitally generated PDFs supplied by the user. The intended users are students and researchers conducting initial paper triage, not systems making autonomous scientific, medical, legal, or financial decisions.

NLP is justified because the central obstacles involve natural-language meaning, discourse structure, and terminology variation rather than file storage alone. Extractive summarization ranks sentences according to patterns of lexical centrality; retrieval matches a natural-language question against candidate passages; research-information extraction maps text to categories such as methodology and findings; and RAG conditions answer production on retrieved evidence. Retrieval-augmented generation was originally proposed to combine pretrained parametric memory with non-parametric memory for knowledge-intensive tasks, which closely matches the need to consult a specified document rather than rely on model memory. [Lewis et al. (2020)](kimi-cite://web?url=https%3A%2F%2Farxiv.org%2Fabs%2F2005.11401 "citation") The prototype’s expected output is a structured research brief, an answer with source identifiers, and inspectable page-level passages. Its value proposition is reduced navigation effort and improved traceability, not replacement of close reading. No time-saving or accuracy improvement for users is claimed because the project did not include a controlled user study.

## 2. Input Data and Annotation Strategy

The application’s primary input is a research paper in PDF format, with each extracted page represented by a one-based page number and a text string. For quantitative evaluation, the pilot corpus contained *Attention Is All You Need*, *Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks*, and *TextRank: Bringing Order into Text*. These papers were selected because they are publicly accessible, relevant to the system’s technical area, and varied in age, length, and structure. They provide 15, 19, and eight pages respectively, yielding 56, 93, and 56 chunks under the fixed configuration. The papers were obtained from arXiv and ACL Anthology, and SHA-256 hashes, landing pages, and local paths were recorded in `data/corpus_manifest.json`. [Vaswani et al. (2017)](kimi-cite://web?url=https%3A%2F%2Farxiv.org%2Fabs%2F1706.03762 "citation") [Mihalcea and Tarau (2004)](kimi-cite://web?url=https%3A%2F%2Faclanthology.org%2FW04-3252%2F "citation") The PDFs remain local evaluation inputs and are excluded from Git redistribution; the repository retains reproducible source URLs and annotations instead of assuming that public download permission implies unrestricted redistribution.

Each paper received one independently paraphrased reference summary and six questions. Five questions were answerable and addressed architecture or approach, datasets, numerical or experimental results, and future work or portability. The sixth requested information not reported in the paper, such as an ImageNet result for the Transformer or SQuAD performance for TextRank, to test insufficient-evidence behavior. Reference answers were prepared from the original documents before reviewing generated answers, avoiding circular evaluation against the system’s own output. Relevance annotation identified every chunk judged sufficient to answer each question; this choice makes recall demanding because a correct answer may require only a subset of all relevant passages. The 1000-character chunk size and 150-character overlap were fixed before relevance IDs were finalized. Consequently, annotations are valid for that configuration but must be revisited when chunk boundaries change. This dependency is recorded explicitly rather than hidden in the evaluation script.

| Paper | Pages | Chunks | Answerable QA | Unanswerable QA | Main evaluation content |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Attention Is All You Need | 15 | 56 | 5 | 1 | Architecture, WMT data, BLEU, hardware, future work |
| Retrieval-Augmented Generation | 19 | 93 | 5 | 1 | Memory components, DPR, RAG variants, QA datasets, generation |
| TextRank | 8 | 56 | 5 | 1 | Graph method, extraction tasks, similarity, datasets, portability |
| **Total** | **42** | **205** | **15** | **3** | **18 annotated Q&A cases** |

The corpus is a **pilot, not a representative benchmark**. Three papers from one research area cannot estimate performance across scientific disciplines, scanned documents, multilingual writing, or complex table-heavy layouts. A single reviewer also prepared the annotations and human ratings, so subjective relevance and correctness judgments may be biased or inconsistent. The small sample is nevertheless useful for verifying that the complete experimental pipeline executes and for locating concrete failures. Question-level bootstrap intervals are reported to make uncertainty visible, but resampling 15 questions cannot compensate for limited paper coverage or dependence between questions from the same document. A fuller study should use 20–50 papers, multiple annotators, adjudicated references, and a held-out configuration set. These additions would support stronger conclusions without changing the underlying application architecture.

## 3. Document Processing and System Architecture

The first processing stage uses `pypdf` to extract selectable text and rejects documents for which no page contains usable text. This explicit failure is preferable to silently returning an empty index for scanned papers. Cleanup removes null characters, joins words split by hyphenation at line endings, normalizes repeated spaces, and limits excessive blank lines. When at least three pages are present, short normalized lines repeated on at least half the pages are removed as likely headers or footers. This heuristic reduces recurring noise but can also delete a legitimately repeated sentence, so it remains conservative and documented. The current implementation does not perform OCR, table reconstruction, figure interpretation, or mathematical notation recovery. Users can inspect the extracted text by PDF page in the interface, making extraction problems visible before they trust downstream outputs.

The second stage divides each page into paragraphs, recognizes common numbered or unnumbered section headings, and creates chunks of at most 1000 characters with up to 150 characters of word-level overlap. Chunks never cross a page boundary, which guarantees that each passage has an exact source-page reference and avoids merging unrelated columns across pages. The trade-off is reduced context for arguments that span pages. Each chunk stores a stable ID, text, section label, and page tuple. Stable IDs make retrieval evaluation and error analysis reproducible, while overlap increases the chance that a sentence split near a boundary appears completely in at least one chunk. Because the prototype handles one paper at a time, exact in-memory retrieval is adequate; FAISS is optional for larger collections rather than a required dependency. FAISS was designed for efficient similarity search and can scale far beyond this pilot, but infrastructure appropriate for billion-scale vectors would add unnecessary complexity here. [Johnson, Douze, and Jégou (2017)](kimi-cite://web?url=https%3A%2F%2Farxiv.org%2Fabs%2F1702.08734 "citation")

![PaperTrail architecture](figures/system-architecture.png)

The architecture separates summarization from question answering after their shared preprocessing path. Summarization selects an abstract when a heading is available and otherwise uses the full extracted document, then fills six research fields through section matching and focus terms. Question answering converts the query into the same lexical or vector representation as the chunks, retrieves the top-k passages, and supplies only those passages to the selected answer generator. Offline generation quotes high-overlap sentences with identifiers such as `[S1]`; optional OpenAI generation receives a strict instruction to use only supplied evidence and acknowledge insufficiency. This separation is important for reliability analysis: a wrong answer can be traced to extraction, retrieval, sentence selection, or external generation rather than blamed on an undifferentiated “model.” It also permits BM25, TF-IDF, dense, and hybrid configurations to share the same evaluation and interface code.

## 4. Model Selection, Embeddings, and Tuning

The summarization baseline is LEAD, which selects the opening sentences and is useful because scientific abstracts already concentrate major contributions. TextRank provides an unsupervised graph-based alternative: sentences become vertices, TF-IDF cosine similarities become weighted edges, and iterative centrality ranking selects important sentences that are then restored to source order. The original TextRank work introduced graph-based ranking for keyword and sentence extraction, making it a suitable classical NLP comparison rather than an arbitrary baseline. [Mihalcea and Tarau (2004)](kimi-cite://web?url=https%3A%2F%2Faclanthology.org%2FW04-3252%2F "citation") The implementation uses damping 0.85, up to 40 iterations, and a convergence tolerance of 10^-6. Optional BART summarization uses bounded word excerpts and hierarchical combination to respect model context limits. BART’s denoising sequence-to-sequence pretraining and strong generation results justify its inclusion as an abstractive option, but it was not run in this pilot and therefore receives no performance claim. [Lewis et al. (2020)](kimi-cite://web?url=https%3A%2F%2Faclanthology.org%2F2020.acl-main.703%2F "citation")

Lexical retrieval includes BM25 and TF-IDF. BM25 combines inverse document frequency, term-frequency saturation, and document-length normalization with `k1 = 1.5` and `b = 0.75`, providing a principled probabilistic retrieval baseline. [Robertson and Zaragoza (2009)](kimi-cite://web?url=https%3A%2F%2Fdoi.org%2F10.1561%2F1500000019 "citation") TF-IDF uses English stop-word removal, unigrams and bigrams, sublinear term frequency, and cosine-like sparse inner products after vector normalization. Dense retrieval optionally loads `all-MiniLM-L6-v2` through Sentence Transformers, normalizes embeddings, and uses inner-product search. Sentence-BERT established that siamese and triplet transformer structures can produce reusable embeddings suitable for cosine-similarity comparison. [Reimers and Gurevych (2019)](kimi-cite://web?url=https%3A%2F%2Faclanthology.org%2FD19-1410%2F "citation") Hybrid retrieval combines BM25 and dense rankings through reciprocal-rank fusion with `k = 60`, avoiding direct addition of incompatible raw scores. [Cormack, Clarke, and Buettcher (2009)](kimi-cite://web?url=https%3A%2F%2Fcormack.uwaterloo.ca%2Fcormacksigir09-rrf.pdf "citation") Dense and hybrid modes remain optional because their model download and memory requirements were outside the measured pilot.

The initial offline generator selected up to four sentences with any query-term overlap, which produced unsupported answers to all three deliberately unanswerable questions. Error analysis led to three changes: conservative plural normalization in lexical tokenization, a minimum query-coverage threshold of 0.25 for quoted sentences, and an abstention when a capitalized named term in the question is absent from all retrieved evidence. The maximum answer length was also reduced to three sentences and obvious attribution noise was filtered. These are transparent heuristic rules, not a learned factuality classifier; a paraphrase can still cause over-refusal, and an unsupported question without a distinctive named entity can still receive an answer. After tuning, TF-IDF was selected as the prototype default because it outperformed BM25 on both aggregate Precision@5 and Recall@5 in this corpus. The choice is provisional: the small sample does not justify claiming that TF-IDF is generally superior, and BM25 remains available for comparison.

| Component | Selected default | Alternatives | Selection rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Summarization | TextRank | LEAD, optional BART | Unsupervised structure; LEAD measured; BART unevaluated |
| Retrieval | TF-IDF | BM25, dense, hybrid | Best aggregate pilot Precision@5 and Recall@5 |
| Embeddings | Sparse unigram/bigram vectors | SBERT MiniLM | Offline reproducibility; semantic option retained |
| Answer generation | Evidence quotation | Evidence-only OpenAI RAG | Local privacy and direct inspectability |
| Evidence count | k = 5 | k = 1 or 3 | Higher recall at the cost of lower precision |

## 5. Experimental Design and Evaluation Measures

The experiment compared LEAD with TextRank and BM25 with TF-IDF using identical documents, chunks, questions, and `k = 5`. Summarization quality was measured with ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, and ROUGE-L F1, implemented using token n-gram overlap and longest-common-subsequence lengths. ROUGE is an established automatic summarization metric, but it measures similarity to reference wording and cannot prove factual correctness or complete coverage. [Lin (2004)](kimi-cite://web?url=https%3A%2F%2Faclanthology.org%2FW04-1013%2F "citation") The reported summarization values are macro averages across the three papers, giving each document equal weight. No BART, dense-retrieval, hybrid-retrieval, or external-LLM result is included because those paths were not executed under the pilot conditions. Retaining empty fields for human review in the raw JSON and storing ratings separately also makes the distinction between system output and human judgment auditable.

Retrieval was evaluated on the 15 answerable questions using Precision@k and Recall@k. For relevant set `R` and the top-k retrieved set `T_k`, precision is `|T_k ∩ R| / k` and recall is `|T_k ∩ R| / |R|`. The unanswerable questions were excluded from these retrieval averages because their relevant sets are empty and precision/recall would be undefined or misleading. They were instead used to measure correct abstention, unsupported answering, and over-refusal. The top-five rankings were reused to calculate k = 1, 3, and 5 without retuning per question. Percentile bootstrap intervals for Precision@5 and Recall@5 used 5,000 question-level resamples with seed 2026. These intervals describe variability in this pilot only; they are not population-level guarantees and do not remove paper-selection or single-annotator bias.

Generated Q&A responses were rated on a 0–2 scale for correctness, relevance, evidence support, and citation accuracy. A score of zero indicated failure, one indicated partial performance, and two indicated complete performance under the rubric in `report/evaluation_rubric.md`. The review covered all 18 outputs, including abstentions, and the raw answer, reference answer, cited pages, rating, and explanatory note remain available for inspection. This human layer is essential because an answer can quote genuine source sentences while still missing the question’s central request. The ratings are subjective and come from one reviewer, so they should be interpreted as structured error analysis rather than reliable human-evaluation research. Future evaluation should add multiple raters, agreement measurement, and adjudication before comparing more complex generators.

## 6. Results and Comparative Analysis

The extractive summarization results were close. LEAD achieved macro ROUGE-1 of **0.334**, ROUGE-2 of **0.092**, and ROUGE-L of **0.186**; TextRank achieved **0.327**, **0.088**, and **0.211** respectively. LEAD therefore led on unigram and bigram overlap, while TextRank led on longest-common-subsequence overlap. The absolute differences are small and based on only three references, so they do not support a general ranking of the algorithms. The results are consistent with the intuition that abstracts already place useful content early, reducing the advantage of centrality ranking. TextRank’s higher ROUGE-L suggests somewhat better preservation of longer reference sequences in this sample, but that interpretation requires more documents and human factuality assessment. Both methods are extractive, so neither can generate a concise paraphrase when the source expresses information across several disconnected sentences.

![Summarization results](figures/summarization-comparison.png)

Retrieval showed a clearer aggregate pattern, although uncertainty remained substantial. TF-IDF and BM25 tied at Precision@1 of 0.467, but TF-IDF was higher at k = 3 and k = 5. At k = 5, TF-IDF achieved precision **0.333** with a 95% bootstrap interval of [0.227, 0.440] and recall **0.678** with an interval of [0.444, 0.878]. BM25 achieved **0.267** [0.187, 0.347] and **0.583** [0.406, 0.761]. The recall gain from k = 1 to k = 5 confirms the expected trade-off: returning more passages covers more annotated evidence but introduces a larger proportion of irrelevant passages. Per-paper TF-IDF recall ranged from 0.600 on TextRank to 0.733 on the Transformer paper, while BM25 ranged from 0.433 to 0.767. This variation reinforces the need for multi-paper reporting instead of presenting one aggregate number as universal.

| Retriever | P@1 | R@1 | P@3 | R@3 | P@5 | R@5 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| BM25 | 0.467 | 0.233 | 0.356 | 0.472 | 0.267 | 0.583 |
| TF-IDF | 0.467 | 0.194 | 0.422 | 0.522 | 0.333 | 0.678 |

The tuning intervention improved abstention but not every outcome. Initially, all three unanswerable questions received unsupported answers; after named-term and coverage checks, all three received the intended insufficient-evidence response and unsupported answers fell to zero. However, one answerable question about future research directions became an over-refusal, giving an over-refusal rate of 1/15 among answerable questions. Conservative plural normalization also failed to improve BM25: its initial P@5/R@5 of 0.280/0.606 changed to 0.267/0.583, while TF-IDF was unchanged. The final results therefore do not present tuning as universally beneficial. They show that reliability rules can trade unsupported answering against missed valid answers and that retrieval changes require measurement rather than intuition.

Human Q&A ratings revealed the cost of a deliberately simple offline generator. Mean correctness and relevance were both **1.39/2**, evidence support was **1.89/2**, and citation accuracy was **1.33/2**. High evidence support reflects the fact that most output consists of verbatim source sentences, whereas lower correctness and relevance reflect incomplete dataset lists, irrelevant additional sentences, and answers that discuss decoding instead of the requested model distinction. For example, one Transformer answer included the English-French training size but omitted English-German; a RAG answer named only two of four QA datasets; and a TextRank answer discussed edge weights without explaining content-overlap similarity. These failures occur after retrieval as well as during it, so increasing recall alone would not solve answer quality. A future generator should select sentences jointly for coverage and redundancy, while retaining the same citations and abstention tests.

## 7. Ethics, Reliability, and Limitations

The principal ethical risk is **unsupported or misleading scientific communication**. Retrieval and citations reduce this risk but do not eliminate it: a passage can be genuine yet irrelevant, incomplete, or extracted incorrectly. PaperTrail mitigates this through page-level sources, full evidence inspection, explicit insufficient-evidence responses, and instructions to verify important claims against the original paper. External generation is optional and receives only retrieved passages under an evidence-only prompt, but prompt compliance is not guaranteed. NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework emphasizes validity, transparency, privacy, human oversight, and evaluation of risks in context, supporting the project’s decision to document failures and intended scope rather than market the prototype as authoritative. [NIST AI RMF](kimi-cite://web?url=https%3A%2F%2Fnvlpubs.nist.gov%2Fnistpubs%2Fai%2Fnist.ai.100-1.pdf "citation") The system should not be used as the sole basis for citations in assessed work, research conclusions, or high-stakes decisions.

Privacy and copyright require separate controls because a locally running interface can still transmit data when external models are enabled. Default summarization, retrieval, and answer quotation run locally after package installation; uploaded documents are not intentionally persisted by application code. If OpenAI mode is selected, the question and retrieved excerpts leave the local machine, so users must not enable it for unpublished or confidential papers without authorization. Source PDFs for the pilot are excluded from version control, and only URLs, hashes, and annotations are retained. This reduces redistribution risk but does not replace a license review for future corpora. Bias also enters through English-only scope, paper selection, pretrained embedding or generator models, and subjective annotation. A larger evaluation should document disciplinary, demographic, language, and open-access coverage and should measure whether extraction failures disproportionately affect particular document formats.

| Risk | Concrete failure mode | Implemented mitigation | Residual limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hallucination | Fluent claim absent from paper | Evidence-only prompt, quotations, abstention | No formal faithfulness guarantee |
| Incomplete evidence | Correct passage omitted at k = 5 | Adjustable k, all-evidence viewer | Higher k reduces precision |
| Privacy | Confidential excerpt sent externally | Local default, explicit external-mode warning | User must enforce organizational policy |
| Copyright | Unauthorized paper redistribution | PDFs ignored by Git, source URLs retained | Users remain responsible for permissions |
| Research integrity | Summary omits limitations or numerical context | Structured fields and source-page verification | No automated completeness assurance |
| Evaluation bias | Subjective references or ratings | Fixed rubric and retained annotations | One rater, three papers, no agreement estimate |

Technical limitations further constrain generalization. Scanned documents require OCR; multi-column layouts can interleave text; tables, equations, and figures may lose meaning; and page-bounded chunks can separate necessary context. Section detection covers common headings but not the full diversity of scientific publishing formats. Sparse retrieval cannot reliably bridge paraphrases, while dense retrieval depends on model quality and resource availability. Named-term abstention addresses only a narrow class of unsupported questions, and coverage thresholds can reject valid paraphrased evidence. Finally, the pilot’s confidence intervals are wide and its human ratings lack independent replication. These are substantive limitations of the evidence as well as the software, and they define the most important future experiments: larger stratified corpora, OCR and layout-aware extraction, cross-page chunking, dense/hybrid evaluation, and independent human reliability analysis.

## 8. Prototype Demonstration and Reproducibility

The working prototype is available as both a Streamlit application and a Python API. The demonstration sequence is to upload a digital English PDF, select TF-IDF and TextRank, process the paper, inspect the overview and six research fields, ask a dataset or methodology question, and open the cited passages to verify their page numbers. A second demonstration should ask a question absent from the paper to show insufficient-evidence behavior, followed by inspection of the extracted page text to discuss layout errors. The browser workflow was exercised with a synthetic PDF, and unit and integration tests cover extraction, chunking, retrieval, summarization, metrics, abstention, and the complete offline pipeline. The accompanying Colab notebook provides an alternative demonstration environment when a local web interface is not required.

```bash
python -m pip install -r requirements.txt
streamlit run app.py
```

Reproduction of the measured pilot uses the manifest, annotations, evaluation runner, and aggregation script. The commands below regenerate per-paper JSON and the aggregate metrics; `node scripts/render_charts.mjs` recreates the figures from the aggregate file. Dependency groups separate the lightweight offline prototype from optional transformer and API integrations, making the default demonstration practical without model training or paid services. The repository also includes the evaluation rubric, raw outputs, initial-versus-tuned results, and a submission checklist. These artifacts make the project auditable: another student can distinguish code behavior, measured evidence, human judgment, and future work instead of encountering only a polished interface and unsupported performance claims.

```bash
python scripts/evaluate.py data/papers/textrank.pdf data/annotations/textrank.json --methods bm25 tfidf --summarizers lead textrank --output results/pilot/textrank.json
python scripts/aggregate_pilot.py
node scripts/render_charts.mjs
```

## 9. Conclusion

PaperTrail satisfies the project’s central technical objective: a working NLP prototype that converts scientific PDFs into structured summaries and document-grounded answers with inspectable page evidence. Its design combines classical extractive summarization and lexical retrieval with optional transformer embeddings, BART, FAISS, and RAG generation, while a lightweight offline path preserves accessibility and privacy. The measured pilot found better aggregate retrieval for TF-IDF than BM25, mixed LEAD/TextRank summarization results, perfect abstention on three unsupported questions, and persistent problems with incomplete answers and one over-refusal. These findings are limited by three papers, 18 questions, and one human rater, so the appropriate conclusion is feasibility with identified reliability risks, not demonstrated general superiority. The most valuable next step is a larger independent evaluation that compares dense and hybrid retrieval and improves evidence selection without weakening source traceability.
